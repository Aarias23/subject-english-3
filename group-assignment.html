GROUP 2 · GRAMMAR STUDIO

Present & Past Simple

  1. JUAN FRANCISCO GARCIA VALERIO
  2. ASHLEY GUZMAN CASTILLO
  3. IVANIA GUADALUPE GUZMAN SORIANO

Guiding questions

  • When do we use the present simple for habits and facts, and the past simple for finished actions?
  • How do we form affirmative sentences, negatives and questions in each tense?
  • What changes with he / she / it? Why do we use the base verb after does, doesn’t, did and didn’t?
  • How do regular and irregular verbs change in the past? Give one example of each.

Model examples: “She studies every day.” / “She studied yesterday.” “She doesn’t study on Sundays.” / “She didn’t study yesterday.” “Does she study?” / “Did she study?”

Simple visual: Create a two-column chart: Every day / Yesterday. Highlight the verbs and time expressions. Include one habit, one finished action and one irregular verb pair such as go → went.

Share the speaking time: Member 1 explains present simple; Member 2 explains past simple; Member 3 compares the forms and leads the class question. Each member gives at least one example.

Ask the class: “Every day, she ___ English. Yesterday, she ___ English.” Use study and explain both answers.

GROUP 3 · GRAMMAR STUDIO

Future Actions: Will & Going To

  1. MARFEL SOFIA MARTINEZ LOPEZ
  2. ANDRY MARIA MATEO AMADOR
  3. FRANCHESCA MORENO VICTORIANO

Guiding questions

  • How do we form affirmative sentences, negatives and questions with will and be going to?
  • When can we use will for a decision made now, an offer or a promise?
  • When can we use going to for an earlier plan or a prediction based on present evidence?
  • Which future time expressions can we use, and why does going to need am, is or are?

Model examples: “I’ll help you.” “I won’t be late.” “Will you call tomorrow?” “We are going to study tonight.” “She isn’t going to travel.” “Are you going to practice?”

Simple visual: Create a Will / Going to comparison chart and a short dialogue. Show a decision made now, a plan made earlier and a prediction with visible evidence.

Share the speaking time: Member 1 explains will; Member 2 explains be going to; Member 3 compares the situations and leads the class question. Each member gives at least one example.

Ask the class: “We booked our tickets yesterday. We ___ travel next week.” Complete the sentence using be going to and explain your choice.

Remember: Will and going to can sometimes overlap. Explain the context instead of treating the difference as an absolute rule.

GROUP 4 · THE QUANTITY STUDIO

Partitives: Portions, Containers & Measures

  1. OSEINY MISEL MOTA MOTA
  2. LUCERO PEREZ SANCHEZ
  3. AXEL BLADIMIR PIMENTEL SOTO

Guiding questions

  • What is a partitive, and how does it name a unit, portion, container or measure”
  • How do we build number + countable unit + of + noun? Which word becomes plural”
  • When can we use slice, piece, loaf, bottle, cup, bag and kilo? Can a unit also measure countable nouns”

Model examples: “A slice of bread.” / “Two slices of bread.” “A piece of advice.” / “Three pieces of advice.” “One bottle of water is enough.” / “Two bottles of water are enough.” “A bag of apples.”

Simple visual: Show pictures or real objects in three categories: portions, containers and measures. Label at least six examples and show loaf → loaves. Keep bread and advice uncountable in these examples.

Share the speaking time: Member 1 explains the pattern and portions; Member 2 presents containers and measures; Member 3 explains plurals and leads the class question. Each member gives at least one example.

Ask the class: “We need two ___ of bread and three ___ of water.” Use slice and bottle, then explain which words become plural.

GROUP 5 · THE QUANTITY STUDIO

Quantifiers: How Much & How Many”

  1. DANYELIS REYES
  2. JOLENNY REYES MEJIA
  3. ENEROLISA RODRÍGUEZ CORDERO

Guiding questions

  • How does the noun help us choose many / much and a few / a little”
  • When do we commonly use some and any? What happens in offers such as “Would you like some tea?”
  • How do a lot of, enough, too many and too much describe large, sufficient or excessive quantities”
  • How does a few / a little differ from few / little? How do we refer to a specific group with most of the or none of the”

Model examples: “How many apples?” / “How much water?” “We have a few apples and a little water.” “We don’t have any milk.” “There is a lot of rice.” “We have enough cups, but too much sugar.” “Most of the students brought water.”

Simple visual: Create a countable / uncountable chart with matching quantifiers. Add a shopping or picnic example to show enough and too much / too many. Use the remaining expressions as brief comparisons.

Share the speaking time: Member 1 explains some / any and many / much; Member 2 explains small and large quantities; Member 3 explains sufficiency, excess and specific groups, then leads the class question. Each member gives at least one example.

Ask the class: “Six guests need one cup each. We have four cups. Do we have enough cups? How many more do we need?” Explain: “We don’t have enough cups. We need two more.”

Remember: A lot of describes a large quantity; too much / too many adds a problem. A few / a little means some, but does not always mean enough. Review all / most / some / none in your study guide.
